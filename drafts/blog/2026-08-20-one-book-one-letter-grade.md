# More is being asked of your library. Here's how to carry it.

If you run a Texas district, you know the feeling: the asks around library books keep arriving, and the people handling them are the same people who handled everything last year. Nobody added staff or budget for this.

So let's set the politics aside — you don't get a vote on it anyway — and talk about what you do control: how your team does this work well, and defensibly, with what it has.

## Two laws, one school year

**SB 13** is the one everybody names. It governs what's on the shelf and how it gets there.

**SB 12** is the one your peers have started asking about. It's easy to miss — its title never mentions libraries; it's about parental rights — but it applies beginning with the same 2025–2026 school year, and in three places it lands on your library.

And one thing in the air: at the Capitol on August 19, Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick said the state will pass legislation next session dropping a district a full letter grade on its A–F rating if prohibited titles remain on shelves, and confirmed one book would be enough. No bill has been filed — a stated intention, not law. But A–F is the measure the state uses when it intervenes, so it's worth knowing where your record stands.

## What SB 13 asks — and the work each ask creates

- **Post, then approve at the board.** Titles go public for 30 days, then get approved in an open meeting — a rolling calendar all year, though same-ISBN replacements and copies are exempt. (TEC §33.026)
- **A challenge blocks access before the review.** Any parent, employee, or resident can file, and students can't reach the title until you act. Drop-everything work. (TEC §33.027)
- **Classroom shelves count.** The policy reaches classroom libraries, catalogs, and apps — anything a student can open. Remove a title, and teachers get notified to pull their copies. (TEC §33.021, §33.027)
- **Decisions need reasons.** Categories are defined, and removal can't rest solely on a book's ideas or who wrote it. Documentation per title — and protection for whoever makes the call. (TEC §33.021)
- **Parents get the catalog.** Catalog access, a do-not-check-out list for their child, and — with a learning management system or portal — a record of each checkout. (TEC §33.023, §33.024)
- **The educational-purpose shield is gone.** SB 412 repealed that defense, so the care above isn't bureaucratic. For staff, it's personal. (Penal Code §43.24)

## Where SB 12 folds in

Three connections are real. One is a coincidence worth dismissing.

**Your annual parent notice now has to mention library materials.** SB 12 requires districts to give every parent — at enrollment and again each year — information on their rights and options, and the list names "instructional materials and library materials" outright, plus the grievance procedure. TEA writes the form; you post it prominently. Every family gets told, in writing, every year, that they have a say and how to use it.

**Library checkout records are a named parental record.** Both bills wrote a library-records entitlement into the same subsection of the parental-records statute in the same session, worded slightly differently. Either way: produce a child's library record when a parent asks.

**Disputes now run on a clock, with a documentation standard.** SB 12 turns the old complaint provision into a formal grievance process: four levels of review from principal to board, a hearing within 10 days of filing, a written decision within 20 days after, standardized forms and electronic filing posted on your site and in the handbook. Two details matter most. A grievance can allege violation of board policy or of anything in Title 2 of the Education Code — where your collection development policy and SB 13's library duties both live. And each decision's record must include an explanation of its basis and an indication of every document supporting it. Districts report all grievances to TEA yearly; lose five appeals at the commissioner in a year and your superintendent testifies before the State Board of Education.

Read together, they want the same thing from different directions: SB 13 a reasoned determination about a book, SB 12 a documented basis for how you handled the dispute about it — on a deadline.

**What SB 12 doesn't do:** change what's allowed on a shelf. No new categories, no change to SB 13's standards. Its new annual certification covers instruction-related sections, not library materials. It does add a section to the same chapter as SB 13's library provisions — but that one's about student clubs.

## The lists still don't explain themselves

Parents are being pointed to advocacy organizations' sites — not TEA, not a state source — to download flagged-title lists and bring them to a board meeting. What arrives is a title and an author. No reason. No category. No page cited. No ISBN, so no way to know which edition. Pulitzer Prize–winning fiction shows up alongside everything else.

That's not what the statute asks for either. SB 13's own challenge form requires whoever files it to identify *how* the title violates the library standards. A bare list doesn't answer that — it just asks it, at volume, on your clock.

The way through isn't faster guessing. It's evidence attached to the specific title, at the edition level, with its source visible — so your team can show how it decided, not just what.

## Where the hours actually go

Four jobs, whether or not you ever use Bookmarked:

1. **Inventory every shelf**, classroom collections included. An uncatalogued shelf can't be reviewed — or defended.
2. **Work at the edition level.** The statute keys its own exemptions to ISBN; a title-and-author string can't tell two editions apart.
3. **Write the reasoning down.** Both laws expect it: who decided, what they reviewed, why.
4. **Keep the record board-ready.** With grievance clocks running, the useful record is one you can produce this week, not reconstruct next month.

## Giving the hours back

That's the work Bookmarked was built to shrink. **Book Intelligence** organizes what's publicly known about a book — from sources outside the book itself — tied to the exact ISBN, so a review doesn't start at a blank search bar. **OnShelf Review** puts that intelligence inside your book order and approval workflow, producing a defensible system of record as a byproduct: who decided what, when, and on what basis. **Classroom Libraries** catalogs every classroom collection alongside the library.

None of this is compliance for its own sake. It's giving your librarians their time back — so the library can be about readers and books, backed by a record you can stand behind.
