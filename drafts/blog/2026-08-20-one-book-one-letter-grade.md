# One book. One letter grade.

## What changed on August 19

At a press conference at the Texas Capitol on August 19, Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick said the state will pass legislation next session dropping a district a full letter grade on its A–F accountability rating if prohibited titles remain on library shelves. Asked directly whether a single book would be enough, he confirmed it would.

"School districts be aware," he said. "Next session, if these books are not off the shelf, we will pass a law that will knock your grade letter down one." To superintendents, he added: "Look at what's in your libraries. Maybe you didn't know. Today, you know."

Two facts put that in proportion. No bill has been filed — as of August 19, this is a stated legislative intention, not law. But the A–F rating is the same measure the state uses when it intervenes in a district. Not yet law, yet aimed at the rating that matters most — which makes this preparation work, not alarm.

Patrick also said 80% of Texas districts are not complying with SB 13 — his figure, not independently verified. The Texas Tribune and outlets statewide carried the account within a day.

## What the law already requires — today

Whatever happens next session, these obligations are in force now, under SB 13 and SB 412:

- **Post, then approve at the board.** Every new or donated title is posted publicly for 30 days, then approved in an open meeting before it reaches a shelf. (TEC §33.021)
- **A challenge blocks access before the review.** The statute requires access blocked district-wide on receipt of a challenge — before the review takes place, not after. Any parent, employee, or district resident may file one. (TEC §33.023)
- **Classroom shelves are in scope.** The requirements reach classroom collections, online catalogs, mobile apps, and — in the statute's own words — "any other library catalog." (TEC §33.021)
- **Determinations need reasoning.** TEA's model policy points "indecent" and "profane" to statutory definitions, and a determination requires a finding of fact with reasoning — for "pervasively vulgar," after reviewing the whole book. (TEC §33.021(d); TEA guidance, Aug. 28, 2025)
- **Parents get the catalog.** A public online catalog, enforceable per-child opt-out lists, and checkout records where a library system or parent portal exists. (TEC §33.024)
- **The educational-purpose shield is gone.** SB 412 repealed the bona fide educational-purpose defense, so staff carry personal legal exposure without it. (Penal Code §43.24)

None of that is proposed. All of it is current law.

## The problem with the lists

From the same podium, parents were pointed to advocacy organizations' websites — not TEA, not a state source — where they can download a list of flagged titles for their district and bring it to a board meeting. Those lists give you a title and an author. That is all. No stated reason. No category. No page or passage cited. No ISBN, so no way to tell which edition is meant. Pulitzer Prize–winning fiction appears on them alongside everything else.

So a board gets two bad options: act on a list that cannot explain itself, or set it aside and carry the risk. The dynamic is already live: per KCBD and EverythingLubbock, a special board meeting in Lubbock ISD the same week drew more than 130 challenges and removed 10 books.

There is a third option: evidence attached to the specific title, at the edition level, with its source visible — so the district can show *how* it decided, not only *what* it decided. That is also the standard the statute's own findings-of-fact language points toward.

And it's worth remembering whose work this is. "School librarians are trained educators who select materials under collection development policies approved by their local school boards, in accordance with SB 13," Texas Library Association Executive Director Shirley Robinson said, adding that "parents are the final voice when deciding what their own children read, but a parent or group should not make those decisions for every family." Whatever a district makes of that debate, the practical point stands: the people doing this work are trained professionals, and they deserve better inputs than a bare list.

## What a district can do now

These steps hold up whether or not you ever use Bookmarked:

1. **Inventory every shelf.** Classroom collections sit outside the central catalog and squarely inside the statute's scope; what isn't catalogued can't be reviewed — or defended.
2. **Work at the edition level.** A title-and-author string can't distinguish editions. Tie every review to an ISBN.
3. **Write the reasoning down.** The statute's findings-of-fact standard is a documentation standard. Record who decided, what they reviewed, and why.
4. **Keep the record board-ready.** The review that protects a district is the one it can produce in an open meeting.

This is the work Bookmarked was built for. **Book Intelligence** organizes everything publicly known about a book — from sources outside the book itself — for decision-making, tied to the exact ISBN, not a title string. **OnShelf Review** is a book order and approval workflow with that intelligence inline, producing a defensible system of record: who decided what, when, and on what basis. **Classroom Libraries** catalogs every classroom collection alongside the library.

The goal isn't compliance for its own sake. It's a library that works for kids — and a decision process a district can stand behind in public, with its library professionals respected and equipped.
