# More requirements. Same staff. Same budget.

Set the politics aside — most district leaders already have, because they don't get a vote on any of it. What's left is an operational question: the requirements around school library books keep growing, the staff and hours doing the work do not, and as of August 19, a single title could someday carry a district's letter grade with it.

Nobody gets to opt out of that math. So this post is about the only part a district controls: how to do this work well, and defensibly, with the people and budget you actually have.

## What grew on August 19

At a press conference at the Texas Capitol, Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick said the state will pass legislation next session dropping a district a full letter grade on its A–F accountability rating if prohibited titles remain on library shelves. Asked directly, he confirmed a single book is enough. "School districts be aware," he said. "Next session, if these books are not off the shelf, we will pass a law that will knock your grade letter down one."

Two facts size it. No bill has been filed — this is a stated intention as of August 19, not law. And the A–F rating is the same measure the state uses when it intervenes in a district. Nothing new is required this morning — but the cost of doing today's work loosely just went up.

## What the law already asks — and the work each ask creates

Whatever happens next session, six obligations are already in force under SB 13 and SB 412 — each a job, not a sentence in a policy manual:

- **Post, then approve at the board.** Every new or donated title is posted publicly for 30 days, then approved in an open meeting before it reaches a shelf. A rolling calendar, every order, all year. (TEC §33.021)
- **A challenge blocks access before the review.** Access is blocked district-wide on receipt — before the review — and any parent, employee, or district resident may file. Every challenge is drop-everything work. (TEC §33.023)
- **Classroom shelves are in scope.** The requirements reach classroom collections, online catalogs, mobile apps, and "any other library catalog" — the cataloguing surface extends past the library walls. (TEC §33.021)
- **Determinations need reasoning.** "Indecent" and "profane" point to statutory definitions; "pervasively vulgar" requires a finding of fact with reasoning, after reviewing the whole book. Documentation work, per title. (TEC §33.021(d); TEA guidance, Aug. 28, 2025)
- **Parents get the catalog.** A public online catalog, enforceable per-child opt-out lists, and checkout records where a library system or parent portal exists. Infrastructure to stand up and keep current. (TEC §33.024)
- **The educational-purpose shield is gone.** SB 412 repealed the bona fide educational-purpose defense — so the care above isn't bureaucratic. For staff, it's personal. (Penal Code §43.24)

None of that is proposed. All of it is on the calendar now.

## The lists don't lighten the load

From the same podium, parents were pointed to advocacy organizations' websites — not TEA, not a state source — where they can download a list of flagged titles for their district and bring it to a board meeting. Operationally, such a list hands a district a title and an author. That is all. No stated reason. No category. No page or passage cited. No ISBN, so no way to tell which edition is meant. Pulitzer Prize–winning fiction appears alongside everything else.

A bare list doesn't answer any of the questions the statute requires a district to answer. It only asks them — at volume.

The way through isn't faster guessing. It's evidence attached to the specific title, at the edition level, with its source visible — showing *how* the district decided, not only *what*. That's also the standard the statute's findings-of-fact language sets.

The people best equipped to weigh that evidence are already in the building. "School librarians are trained educators who select materials under collection development policies approved by their local school boards, in accordance with SB 13," Texas Library Association Executive Director Shirley Robinson said. Give trained professionals better inputs than a bare list, and the work gets smaller and more defensible at once.

## Where the hours actually go

Four jobs, whether or not you ever use Bookmarked:

1. **Inventory every shelf.** Classroom collections sit outside the central catalog and squarely inside the statute; an uncatalogued shelf can't be reviewed — or defended.
2. **Work at the edition level.** A title-and-author string can't distinguish editions. Tie every review to an ISBN.
3. **Write the reasoning down.** The findings-of-fact standard is a documentation standard: who decided, what they reviewed, and why.
4. **Keep the record board-ready.** The review that protects a district is the one it can produce in an open meeting.

## Giving the hours back

That's the work Bookmarked was built to shrink. **Book Intelligence** organizes everything publicly known about a book — from sources outside the book itself — tied to the exact ISBN, so a review doesn't start from a blank search bar. **OnShelf Review** is a book order and approval workflow with that intelligence inline, and the byproduct is a defensible system of record: who decided what, when, and on what basis. **Classroom Libraries** catalogs every classroom collection alongside the library, closing the gap the statute already counts.

The point was never compliance for its own sake. It's giving librarians their time back — so the library can be about readers and books, backed by a decision record the district can stand behind in public.

Bookmarked informs. Districts decide.
