# Your team already asked the right question

Somewhere in your district last week, a librarian finished reading the news out of Austin and said two words: *which books?*

That's not resistance. That's the job. It's the same question your board's collection development policy has been asking since the day it was adopted, and it's what every review comes down to in the end. Last week it just got asked a lot louder.

Here's the news that prompted it. At the Capitol on August 19, Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick said the state will pass legislation next session that drops a district a full letter grade on its A–F rating if prohibited titles remain on its shelves, and confirmed that one title would be enough. No bill has been filed — this is a stated intention, not law. But A–F is the number your community already watches, so it's reasonable to want to know where your record would stand.

Which puts us back at those two words.

No one is going to send you the list. Under SB 13 the standards live in your board's policy and the call lives with your district — that's the design, and it's the part that protects your staff. What does arrive is a different kind of list: a printout from an advocacy organization's website, brought to public comment by a parent who was told this is how you help. Titles and authors, several pages of them.

Nobody in that exchange is acting in bad faith. The parent read something that worried them and did what they were told would help. Your board wants to give a real answer. And your librarian, two rows back, is already doing the arithmetic on what those pages just became.

So look closely at what that page can actually tell you. A title. An author. That's it. No reason. No category from your policy. No page or passage cited. No ISBN — so no way to know which edition, or whether the copy on your shelf is the copy someone read about.

That's not a knock on the parent. It's an honest read of what the document contains. And it's worth noting that SB 13 asks for more than that as well: the state's challenge form requires the person filing it to identify *how* the title violates the library standards. A bare list doesn't answer that question. It asks it — at volume, on your calendar.

So the way through isn't reviewing faster. It's having something real attached to the specific title before the conversation starts.

## Four things make that possible

Whether or not Bookmarked is ever involved:

1. **Know what you have.** Every shelf, classroom collections included. A book nobody has catalogued can't be reviewed, and it can't be defended either.
2. **Work at the edition level.** SB 13 keys its own acquisition exemptions to ISBN. A title-and-author string can't tell two editions apart; an ISBN can.
3. **Write down the reasoning.** Not just the outcome — the reasoning. Who looked, what they looked at, which part of your policy applied.
4. **Keep it board-ready.** The useful record is the one you can put in front of your board this week, not reconstruct next month.

That list is also a fair description of where your library team's hours go once a season like this starts. None of it is the work they signed up for, and none of it arrived with new staff or new money.

## Giving the hours back

That's the part Bookmarked was built to shrink. **Book Intelligence** organizes what's publicly known about a book — from sources outside the book itself — tied to the exact ISBN, so a review doesn't start at an empty search bar. **OnShelf Review** puts that inside your book order and approval workflow, so the record of who decided what, when, and on what basis is a byproduct of doing the work instead of a second job. **Classroom Libraries** brings classroom collections into the same view as the library.

The goal isn't compliance for its own sake. It's giving your librarians their time back, so the library can be about readers and books — with a record behind it you'd be glad to stand on.

Bookmarked informs. Districts decide.
