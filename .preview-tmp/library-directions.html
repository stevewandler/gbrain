Three ways the library could look

Same screen, same database. Every cover, title, author and flag count below is pulled live from the Ashwell Creek High School collection — nothing here needs a field we do not already store.

The current page is one long alphabetical grid. Each direction below answers a different question about what a librarian opened it to do.

A

Worklist

A dense, sortable queue built for getting through review season. Ranked by how heavily a title has been challenged elsewhere, with multi-select so a decision can be applied to forty books at once instead of forty times.

Best for the two weeks before a board meeting.

Works because
The bar makes the queue rank itself — you see the shape of the work without reading a number. Bulk actions turn the most repetitive job in the product into one gesture. Fits roughly three times more titles per screen.
Costs you
It reads like a spreadsheet. Covers shrink to thumbnails, which is a real loss when browsing rather than working. Least impressive of the three in a first demo.
B

Shelf

Covers at full strength, chrome stripped back. Status becomes a coloured spine down the left edge of each book, and the challenge count appears only when there is one.

Best for the first thirty seconds of a demo, and for browsing a collection rather than processing it.

Ashwell Creek High School52 titles · 52 copies
Works because
A library should look like books. The spine colour carries status without a single label, so the grid stays quiet until something needs attention — and it is the only direction that gets more attractive as the collection grows.
Costs you
Low information density. Bans and challenges collapse into one number. Weak for the review workflow, which is what customers actually buy.
C

Triage

The collection grouped by how much challenge history a title carries, rather than by the alphabet. Three bands, each with its own count, so the page opens by telling a librarian where her attention is owed.

Best for walking a superintendent through the collection — it answers "how exposed are we?" before anyone asks.

Works because
It is the only direction that puts a point of view on screen. The band counts are the sentence a librarian repeats to a principal, and the grouping is computed from data we already hold — no new fields, no new workflow.
Costs you
The thresholds are ours, and we would have to defend them. Horizontal scrolling hides titles. Grouping by review status instead would be more useful day to day, but that needs the review workflow seeded first — today every title reads as Allowed.

My read: A is the one that changes a librarian's week; B is the one that wins a demo. They are not exclusive — the same data supports a view toggle, which the page already has for grid and list. C is the strongest idea and the least ready, because its bands only mean something once titles carry real review decisions rather than a default of Allowed.

Nothing above needs a new API field. Bulk actions and the review states in C need backend work that does not exist yet; everything else is presentation.
