## Cover note

Subject: **The book-list question you asked, with the sources attached**

[NAME] —

You asked whether we could tell you if a book might violate state law. I want to
be straight with you about what we can and can't do there, because the honest
version is more useful to you than the confident one.

**We can't tell you a book violates the law.** Nobody at a vendor should. That
determination belongs to your board, on the advice of your superintendent, under
the standards your district adopted. A tool that answers it for you is a tool
that will eventually answer it wrong, in public, with your name on it.

**What we can do is build the record you'd need to defend the answer either
way.** For each title: which statutory question it touches, what the evidence
actually says, what it doesn't say, where the gap is — and a citation for every
claim, so anything in the report can be read aloud and checked.

That distinction matters more than it used to. Under SB 12 a parent grievance
runs on a clock, and under SB 412 the educational-purpose defense is gone. In
that setting, an answer with no sources behind it isn't a shortcut — it's an
exposure. When you paste a list into a general-purpose AI tool, you get fluent
prose and no receipts. That's the gap we're closing.

### What's in the attached chart

Two separate things, kept separate on purpose:

**1. Known-list flags — has this title been challenged or removed somewhere, and
who says so.** Every flag carries the list, the jurisdiction, the date, and a
link. A flag is a record of what someone else did. It is not a statement about
what's in the book, and we don't let it pretend to be.

**2. Content evidence — what credible sources actually describe about the
content**, mapped to the statutory question it bears on. Professional review
journals, state and district records, staff-reported news. Not social media, not
retail listings, not reader reviews.

An em dash in a column means *no content evidence was found in the sources
reviewed on the run date.* It does not mean the book is fine. It's a statement
about the sources, not about the book, and we write it that way every time.

### What we've measured, and what we haven't

I'd rather you hear the limits from me than find them yourself.

**Measured.** The citation quality of the flag layer, scored against an
independent credibility rubric. On our test set: zero award pages or booklists
miscited as ban evidence, zero social-media or retail sources, a date on 91% of
flags and a named jurisdiction on 51%. That's ten titles and 35 flags — a small
sample, and I'm giving you the denominator so you can weigh it accordingly.

**Not measured.** How accurate the content-evidence half is. There's no
adjudicated benchmark for it yet, so we make no accuracy claim about it. Read
those findings as what the cited sources say, and check the sources. We're
building that benchmark; I'll send you the number when it exists rather than an
adjective now.

**One known defect, marked in the report.** Some citations point at pages whose
contents rotate — a "current top ten" index, for instance — so a claim about a
specific past year can land on a page that no longer shows it. The underlying
claims are documented; the links need repointing. Those carry a note beside them.
I'd rather hand you a marked flaw than let you find it in front of a board.

### What I need from you

Your list, in whatever form you have it. Ideally:

| Title | Author | ISBN (if you have it) | Grade band it's shelved for |
|---|---|---|---|

ISBN matters more than it looks: it's how we avoid the classic failure of
matching the wrong edition or the wrong book with a similar title. And a
title-only list has a real failure mode — a typo silently returns nothing, which
looks identical to "nothing found." We'd rather match on ISBN and tell you when
we can't.

Send it however's easiest. I'll run it and send back the chart plus the
per-title evidence.
